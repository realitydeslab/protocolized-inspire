# Protocolized Inspire — catalog

A catalog of protocol art: works in which an artist or designer writes a protocol — instructions, a score, a contract, a game, a smart contract — that shapes what a group of people does, and that group behaviour is the work. From 1920s instructions and Fluxus scores to smart-contract art and DAOs, compiled by Reality Design Lab as idea material. Each work lists its core idea, its rules, what the collective did, and links to images, video, texts and contracts.

## Scores & Instructions

The artist writes instructions; other people carry them out, and each execution is the work. From telephone paintings and Fluxus event scores to Sol LeWitt's wall drawings.

### Instructions for Execution

Works that exist as a set of instructions and are made, again and again, by assistants, owners or institutions.

#### Unhappy Readymade — Marcel Duchamp (1919)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Post & mail · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness, Time, decay & death
- Idea: Mail the rule, let others build it and let the weather finish it.
- What it is: A wedding present sent by letter from Buenos Aires: Duchamp told his sister Suzanne and her husband Jean Crotti in Paris to hang a geometry textbook on their balcony. Wind, rain and sun then worked on the book; only Suzanne's photograph and her painting of it survive.
- Protocol: By letter, Duchamp instructed the couple to hang a geometry book outside by strings, 'so that its pages would be torn apart and its text would be erased by the elements' (Philadelphia Museum of Art). He did not touch the object; the recipients built it and the weather 'chose its own problems'.
- What the collective did: Suzanne Duchamp and Jean Crotti carried out the instruction on their Paris balcony; Suzanne photographed the weathered book and painted Le Readymade malheureux de Marcel (1920). The only realisation that exists is theirs, known through her photograph.
- Images: https://iiif.micr.io/drOqS/full/1200,/0/default.jpg
- Project page: https://philamuseum.org/collection/object/138592

#### Telephone Pictures (EM 1, EM 2, EM 3) — László Moholy-Nagy (1923)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score
- Idea: If a picture can be dictated, the artist's job is the specification, not the hand.
- What it is: Three porcelain-enamel paintings on steel with the same geometric composition at three sizes, produced by an enamel sign factory in Weimar from Moholy-Nagy's specifications. He later wrote that he placed the order by telephone.
- Protocol: Moholy-Nagy drew the composition on graph paper and used the factory's colour chart; he dictated the positions and colours to the factory supervisor, who held the same chart and paper. The factory, not the artist, made the three versions (large, medium, small).
- What the collective did: Enamel workers at the factory fabricated the paintings from the specification. The telephone story has been questioned (his first wife Lucia Moholy recalled that he visited the factory in person), but the works were shown in 1924 at Der Sturm in Berlin as proof that a picture could be made from transmitted instructions.

- Project page: https://www.moma.org/collection/works/147626

#### Dance Constructions (Huddle, Slant Board and others) — Simone Forti (1961)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Give a group one physical task and the dance emerges from how their bodies solve it together.
- What it is: Task-based pieces from 1960–61 for small groups, such as Huddle, in which seven to nine performers take turns climbing over the group's own tightly embracing bodies, and Slant Board, in which performers move across an inclined plywood ramp using ropes.
- Protocol: Huddle: seven to nine people stand in a tight cluster; one at a time, a performer climbs over the top of the group and rejoins it, and the next begins. Slant Board: three or four performers move up, down and across a steep board holding onto ropes, for about ten minutes.
- What the collective did: Performed first in 1961 in New York lofts, the works have been re-performed by many groups. MoMA acquired them in 2015 as its first historical dance works; the acquisition includes Forti's teaching of new performers, and licences allow Huddle to be staged in several places at once.
- Text: https://doi.org/10.1080/01472526.2021.2024724 (Dance Chronicle)

- Project page: https://www.moma.org/collection/works/200115

#### Statements / Declaration of Intent — Lawrence Weiner (1968)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Contract & certificate
- Idea: Publish the work as language and let whoever holds it choose to build it, have it built, or leave it unbuilt.
- What it is: Weiner's first book, published by Seth Siegelaub and the Louis Kellner Foundation in 1968: 24 short statements describing materials and processes, each a complete work. It came with his rule that the receiver decides whether a work is built at all.
- Protocol: Declaration of Intent: '1. The artist may construct the piece. 2. The piece may be fabricated. 3. The piece need not be built. Each being equal and consistent with the intent of the artist the decision as to condition rests with the receiver upon the occasion of receivership.'
- What the collective did: Collectors, curators, sign painters and readers realise the statements in different forms: painted on walls, installed as objects, or kept only as text. The book itself was printed in an edition of 1,000 and circulated in place of an exhibition of objects.
- Text: https://toninieditore.com/shop-studio-bruno-tonini/p/lawrence-weiner-statements (Artist's book)

#### Wall Drawing #1: Drawing Series II 18 (A & B) — Sol LeWitt (1968)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Contract & certificate
- Idea: Move the drawing off the paper and onto any wall, so that it can be redrawn anywhere by anyone who follows the system.
- What it is: LeWitt's first wall drawing, made directly on the wall of Paula Cooper Gallery in New York in October 1968 for a benefit exhibition against the Vietnam War. Two 48-inch squares of graphite lines follow one permutation from his Drawing Series II.
- Protocol: The drawing is defined by a system: lines in four directions (vertical, horizontal, diagonal left, diagonal right) layered in a fixed combination within divided squares, in hard graphite directly on the wall. The owner holds the certificate and diagram; the physical drawing is painted out after each showing and redrawn for the next.
- What the collective did: First drawn at the Paula Cooper benefit organised by Lucy Lippard and Robert Huot; now owned by SFMOMA (Doris and Donald Fisher Collection), which must have it redrawn each time it is installed. Every installation is a new hand-made instance of the same instruction.
- Text: https://www.cahiersdartinstitute.org/catalogues/sol-lewitt-wall-drawings/description (Cahiers d'Art Institute)
- Images: https://d1hhug17qm51in.cloudfront.net/www-media/2026/08/20201534/FC.474_01_H02-web.jpg

#### Art by Telephone — David H. Katzive (1969)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: Restrict the channel to a phone call and the museum becomes the artist's hands.
- What it is: An exhibition at the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago (1 November–14 December 1969) in which artists in the US and Europe phoned in instructions for works that museum staff then made; the catalogue was an LP of the calls.
- Protocol: Artists communicate their proposal to the curator by telephone only; drawings, blueprints and written descriptions are avoided. MCA staff carry out the works from the spoken instructions. The idea takes Moholy-Nagy's 1922 'telephone pictures' as its starting point.
- What the collective did: Staff produced a Sol LeWitt wall drawing from his oral instructions and poured cement down a slope for Robert Smithson; visitors filled Arman's Plexiglas container with their own trash, and dialled Wolf Vostell's changing phone numbers for instructions for one-minute happenings. The exhibition was restaged as Art by Telephone... Recalled from 2012.
- Images: https://mcachicago.org/wp-content/uploads/image/IIARY2TY/original.jpg
- Project page: https://mcachicago.org/exhibitions/1969/art-by-telephone

#### Commissioned Paintings — John Baldessari (1969)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Contract & certificate, Co-authorship & derivatives
- Idea: Author the procedure, not the painting, and put the maker's name on the work.
- What it is: A series of paintings of a hand pointing at things, each painted by a different amateur painter from Baldessari's photographs, with "A PAINTING BY" and the painter's name lettered below by a sign painter.
- Protocol: A friend points at things Baldessari finds interesting; the pointing is photographed; amateur painters are commissioned to copy the photographs in paint; a sign painter adds the caption "A PAINTING BY" followed by the painter's name.
- What the collective did: Each commissioned painter brought their own skill and style, so the series varies from canvas to canvas while no stroke is Baldessari's. It answered Al Held's remark that conceptual art was 'just pointing at things'.
- Images: https://res.cloudinary.com/smimagebank/image/upload/w_1200,c_limit,f_jpg,fl_progressive/v1586025521/sprueth_magers_John_Baldessari_The_Commissioned_Paintings_9033.jpg https://res.cloudinary.com/smimagebank/image/upload/w_1200,c_limit,f_jpg,fl_progressive/v1586025526/sprueth_magers_John_Baldessari_The_Commissioned_Paintings_9034.jpg

#### Wall Drawing #11 — Sol LeWitt (1969)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: A four-word vocabulary of line directions is enough to generate a whole family of works that other hands can draw.
- What it is: An early pencil wall drawing: a wall split into four parts, each filled with three of the four basic line directions laid over one another. It was first drawn at Paula Cooper Gallery in 1969 by Jerry Orter, the artist Adrian Piper and LeWitt.
- Protocol: 'A wall divided horizontally and vertically into four equal parts. Within each part, three of the four kinds of lines are superimposed.' Drafters work in hard black pencil, spacing the lines by bundles of taped leads.
- What the collective did: The first drafters included Adrian Piper, then LeWitt's assistant and later a major conceptual artist. For the MASS MoCA retrospective (2008) it was redrawn by the LeWitt studio team; the drawing now belongs to Yale University Art Gallery.
- Images: https://massmoca.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/11-1.jpg
- Project page: https://massmoca.org/event/walldrawing11/

#### Wall Drawing #118: 50 randomly placed points connected by straight lines — Sol LeWitt (1971)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness
- Idea: A one-sentence rule with a random step: the same text yields a different drawing every time.
- What it is: A wall drawing that LeWitt wrote for students of the School of the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, in 1971 instead of giving a lecture. Fifty points placed by the drafters are all connected to one another, so each version produces a different dense web.
- Protocol: 'On a wall surface, any continuous stretch of wall, using a hard pencil, place fifty points at random. The points should be evenly distributed over the area of the wall. All of the points should be connected by straight lines.'
- What the collective did: First executed by students in Boston without the artist present. The drafters choose where the points go, and because every point connects to every other, small choices change the whole web. The Art Institute of Chicago received the work in 2023.
- Text: https://www.cahiersdartinstitute.org/catalogues/sol-lewitt-wall-drawings/description (Cahiers d'Art Institute)

#### Wall Drawing #260: On Black Walls, All Two-Part Combinations of White Arcs from Corners and Sides, and White Straight, Not-Straight, and Broken Lines — Sol LeWitt (1975)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: When the title is the whole rule, anyone with the title can rebuild the work.
- What it is: A room-size drawing in white crayon on black walls that works through every two-part combination of arcs and lines inside a grid. The title is the complete instruction.
- Protocol: 'On black walls, all two-part combinations of white arcs from corners and sides, and white straight, not straight, and broken lines.' A team of trained drafters lays out the grid and draws each combination by hand in crayon on painted walls.
- What the collective did: MoMA owns the instructions; the drawing was carried out by a team of assistants for the 1978 LeWitt retrospective and again for 'Focus: Sol LeWitt' (2008–09), covering about 1,500 square feet. LeWitt compared his role to a composer's and the drafters' to musicians'.

#### Wall Drawing #273: Lines to Points on a Grid — Sol LeWitt (1975)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness
- Idea: Fix where lines start and leave where they end to the person drawing.
- What it is: Seven walls with a pencil grid, each carrying red, yellow and blue crayon lines drawn from corners, sides or centre to points on the grid. LeWitt fixed the starting points; the drafters decide how many lines and where they end.
- Protocol: 'A 6-inch (15 cm) grid covering the walls. Lines from corners, sides, and center of the walls to random points on the grid.' Red lines come from the midpoints of the sides, blue from the corners, yellow from the centre, combined differently on each of the seven walls; 'the number of lines and their length are determined by the draftsman.'
- What the collective did: Every installation is a new set of choices by a new team of drafters, so no two versions match. SFMOMA owns the work and publishes a family activity inviting the public to draw their own version from the instructions.
- Images: https://d1hhug17qm51in.cloudfront.net/www-media/2026/08/19121212/FC.512_10N_LEWITT_512_V1R3-web.jpg
- Project page: https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/FC.512/

#### Wall Drawing #289 — Sol LeWitt (1976)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: An instruction can set a drawing problem that the person executing it has to solve.
- What it is: White crayon lines on four black walls with a six-inch pencil grid: lines radiate from the centre, the midpoints of the sides and the corners to points the drafters choose. It is one of the last of LeWitt's 'location' drawings.
- Protocol: 'A 6-inch (15 cm) grid covering each of the four black walls. White lines to points on the grids. Fourth wall: twenty-four lines from the center, twelve lines from the midpoint of each of the sides, twelve lines from each corner. (The length of the lines and their placement are determined by the drafter.)'
- What the collective did: First installed at the Detroit Institute of Arts and, fourth wall only, at MoMA in 1978; owned by the Whitney Museum. At MASS MoCA drafters test each line with red string before drawing it in crayon, deciding the endpoints themselves.
- Images: https://massmoca.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/289-1.jpg
- Project page: https://massmoca.org/event/walldrawing289/

#### "Untitled" (Death by Gun) — Felix Gonzalez-Torres (1990)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Circulation & passing on
- Idea: A monument that works by being carried away, sheet by sheet, into other people's rooms.
- What it is: A stack of identical offset sheets on the floor, each reproducing Time magazine's portraits of the 460 people killed by gunfire in the United States in the week of 1–7 May 1989; visitors take sheets and the stack is refilled.
- Protocol: Print on paper, endless copies, shown as a stack on the floor (about nine inches high). The public may take sheets to keep, display or pass on; the owner reprints and replenishes the stack.
- What the collective did: Visitors carry the sheets out of the museum, so the work is distributed into homes and offices while the stack in the gallery is kept whole by staff. The piece belongs to MoMA and was shown in the Hammer Museum's 2014 exhibition Take It or Leave It.

#### "Untitled" (Placebo) — Felix Gonzalez-Torres (1991)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Time, decay & death
- Idea: Scale a take-away rule to a whole floor and the audience becomes the force that erodes it.
- What it is: A floor carpet of silver-wrapped candies with an ideal weight of 1,000–1,200 lb; visitors may take candies, and the carpet is replenished.

#### do it — Hans Ulrich Obrist (1993)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Co-authorship & derivatives, Open participation
- Idea: Ship an exhibition as a book of scores and let every host make its own version.
- What it is: An exhibition made only of artists' written instructions, begun in Paris in 1993 by Hans Ulrich Obrist with Christian Boltanski and Bertrand Lavier; each venue realises the instructions anew and the show is dismantled afterwards.
- Protocol: Artists send instructions instead of objects. The host institution, its staff and local public interpret and carry them out; the resulting works are not kept after the show, and each new venue starts again from the texts.
- What the collective did: The first twelve instructions were translated into nine languages and circulated as a book. Organised as a touring show by Independent Curators International, do it had been staged in at least 50 locations by about 2013, from Australia and China to Mexico and Uruguay, and grew to hundreds of instruction sets; in 2020 a do it (home) edition invited people to realise instructions at home.
- Images: https://curatorsintl.org/images/Scan_130107_0015_WEB.jpg?w=2000
- Project page: https://curatorsintl.org/exhibitions/18072-do-it-2013

#### Wall Drawing #797 — Sol LeWitt (1995)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Circulation & passing on, Co-authorship & derivatives
- Idea: A relay of imperfect copies turns human error into the drawing.
- What it is: A wall drawing written for four drafters who copy each other's line in turn, from the top of the wall to the bottom. The small errors of each copy accumulate into a rippling field of black, red, yellow and blue lines.
- Protocol: 'The first drafter has a black marker and makes an irregular horizontal line near the top of the wall. Then the second drafter tries to copy it (without touching it) using a red marker. The third drafter does the same, using a yellow marker. The fourth drafter does the same using a blue marker. Then the second drafter followed by the third and fourth copies the last line drawn until the bottom of the wall is reached.'
- What the collective did: Conceived with student participation in mind and first drawn in 1995 by four Amherst College sculpture students at the Mead Art Museum. At MASS MoCA the head drafter based the first line on the Berkshire hills; each copied line took ten to twenty minutes.
- Images: https://massmoca.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/797-1.jpg
- Project page: https://massmoca.org/event/walldrawing797/

#### ORGAN²/ASLSP (Halberstadt performance) — John Cage (2001)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Time, decay & death, Open participation
- Idea: A score that only says 'as slow as possible' can hand a single performance to many generations.
- What it is: A performance of Cage's organ piece "As Slow As Possible" in the St. Burchardi church in Halberstadt, Germany, planned to last 639 years, from 2001 to 2640; a chord changes only every few months or years.
- Protocol: Cage's 1987 score leaves the tempo to the performers with the instruction "as slow as possible"; the Halberstadt organizers set the length at 639 years and a timetable of note changes, which volunteers carry out by adding or removing pipes and weights on a purpose-built organ.
- What the collective did: The performance began on 5 September 2001 with a long rest; the foundation behind it is funded partly by people who sponsor individual years, and each sound change, such as those on 5 February and 5 August 2024, draws crowds to the church to watch a new chord begin.

#### Sol LeWitt: A Wall Drawing Retrospective (MASS MoCA) — Sol LeWitt (2008)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Co-authorship & derivatives, Time, decay & death
- Idea: A retrospective of a dead artist can be newly made by the living, from instructions alone.
- What it is: A long-term installation of 105 LeWitt wall drawings from 1969 to 2007 on nearly an acre of walls in Building 7 at MASS MoCA, North Adams. LeWitt planned it before his death in 2007; others drew all of it.
- Protocol: Each drawing's certificate, diagram and written instructions from the LeWitt estate and Yale University Art Gallery guide the drafters; walls were built to LeWitt's specifications, and damaged drawings are replaced or redrawn under the estate's supervision.
- What the collective did: Over six months, more than 60 artists and art students drew the 105 works, led by LeWitt's senior drafters, in a collaboration of MASS MoCA, Yale University Art Gallery and the Williams College Museum of Art. Drafters' decisions, from line spacing to endpoints, differ from earlier installations of the same drawings.

#### 4′33″ — John Cage (1952)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness, Open participation
- Idea: Frame a stretch of time and let whatever the room and the audience produce become the music.
- What it is: A score in three movements, each marked tacet, for any instrument or combination of instruments; the performers make no intended sound for four minutes and thirty-three seconds.

#### Aus den sieben Tagen — Karlheinz Stockhausen (1968)

#### Routine — Allan Kaprow (1973)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Repeat an everyday gesture between two people until communication breaks down, and notice what you were doing.
- What it is: A five-part score for pairs of participants using mirrors and telephones, realised at the Portland Center for the Visual Arts in December 1973 and later published as an activity booklet (1975).
- Protocol: Part 1 of the programme: "standing somewhere / facing a friend holding a large mirror / trying to catch one's reflection ... moving apart again and again / repeating process / until it's no longer possible to see oneself." Parts 2 and 4 use the telephone, part 5 both.

#### Planetary Dance — Anna Halprin (1981)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: A score simple enough for any community to adopt, with one shared core and a local theme.
- What it is: A community dance ritual built around a movement score called the Earth Run, created in 1981 on Mount Tamalpais in California and performed each year by groups around the world.

#### Let us keep our own noon — David Horvitz (2013)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Time, decay & death
- Idea: Dispersing a crowd can redistribute time: one shared signal splits into many private ones.
- What it is: Forty-seven hand bells cast from a melted French church bell of 1742, rung by forty-seven participants at local solar noon; the ringers then walk away from each other until each hears only their own bell.
- Protocol: At local noon, the exact midpoint between sunrise and sunset, forty-seven participants begin ringing the bells together, then disperse in different directions, ringing, until they can hear no bell but their own. The title comes from a 19th-century protest against railway standard time.

#### 18 Happenings in 6 Parts — Allan Kaprow (1959)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Hand the audience a timetable and they become part of the choreography.
- What it is: An evening at the Reuben Gallery in New York, October 1959, in which three rooms of plastic-sheet walls hosted simultaneous actions, and the audience moved between rooms on printed instructions.
- Protocol: Guests received a programme and three stapled cards: "The performance is divided into six parts... Each part contains three happenings which occur at once. #### Instruction Paintings (Instructions for Paintings) — Yoko Ono (1961)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: Replace the painting with the instruction, and let the viewer finish it with their feet, hands or mind.
- What it is: Paintings that exist as instructions. At AG Gallery, New York, in 1961 Ono showed more than fifteen that were completed by the artist, by visitors or by the environment; at Sogetsu Art Center, Tokyo, in May 1962 she showed only the instructions, handwritten in Japanese by Toshi Ichiyanagi.
- Protocol: Each work is a short instruction; in Painting to Be Stepped On, for example, a scrap of canvas lies on the floor and becomes complete as footprints accumulate. Ono had the Sogetsu instructions written in another person's hand so the work would not depend on her own.
- What the collective did: Visitors walked on, added to or imagined the paintings; Ono communicated most of the AG Gallery instructions by word of mouth. The 1962 Tokyo show made the instruction alone the exhibited work, a step toward conceptual art.

#### Yard — Allan Kaprow (1961)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Open participation, Instruction & score
- Idea: An environment is a score: the materials and the permission to move them are the work.
- What it is: An environment of hundreds of used car tyres heaped in the sculpture garden of the Martha Jackson Gallery, New York, for visitors to walk on, climb and throw around; it has since been remade many times.

#### Grapefruit — Yoko Ono (1964)

- Idea: A book can be a portable exhibition that readers stage themselves.
- What it is: A small self-published book of more than 150 instruction pieces in five sections (Music, Painting, Event, Poetry, Object), issued in Tokyo on 4 July 1964 in an edition of 500. Each page is a work that the reader performs, in action or in the mind.

#### Spatial Poem — Mieko Shiomi (1965)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Post & mail · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Circulation & passing on
- Idea: Post the same instruction to a network and the replies form one poem spread across the globe.
- What it is: A mail project (1965–75) of nine text scores that Shiomi posted to people around the world; their reports of what they did came back by mail and were compiled into maps, objects and a book.

#### Mend Piece — Yoko Ono (1966)

#### A Scratch Orchestra: Draft Constitution — Cornelius Cardew (1969)

- What it is: A constitution, published in The Musical Times in June 1969, for an orchestra open to anyone, founded in London by Cornelius Cardew, Michael Parsons and Howard Skempton.

#### Sonic Meditations — Pauline Oliveros (1971)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: Make listening to each other the instruction, and a room of strangers becomes one instrument.
- What it is: A set of text scores (published 1974) for listening, breathing and sounding together, which any group can perform without musical training.

### Constraints, Chance & Games

Rule systems, constraints and chance procedures that generate outcomes the author does not control.

#### Imaginary Landscape No. 4 (March No. 2) — John Cage (1951)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Chance & randomness, Instruction & score
- Idea: Script the hands on the dials precisely and let the broadcasts of the day fill in the sound.
- What it is: A piece for twelve radios and twenty-four performers plus a conductor: at each radio one player turns the tuning dial and the other the volume, following times and settings fixed by chance.
- Protocol: Cage used chance operations derived from the I Ching to fix, for each pair of players, the wavelengths, volume levels and durations; the score controls the actions but not what the stations are broadcasting.
- What the collective did: First performed at Columbia University's McMillin Theater on 2 May 1951 with Cage conducting; every later performance, from student ensembles to groups such as DIY Space for London, picks up different programmes, so the same score sounds different in each city and hour.
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yV-kWU1Z5u8
- Project page: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_Landscape_No._4_(March_No._2)

#### Concert for Piano and Orchestra — John Cage (1958)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Chance & randomness, Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Give every player a separate rulebook and let the ensemble's overlap be unplanned.
- What it is: An indeterminate concert piece in which the pianist and each orchestral player choose which pages of their parts to play, and the conductor's arms act as a clock rather than a beat.
- Protocol: Parts are independent: the piano solo is a book of graphs in many different notations, any number of the orchestral parts may be used, and performers may play any amount of their material; the conductor moves the arms like clock hands to vary the passage of time.
- What the collective did: At the premiere at New York's Town Hall on 15 May 1958 the audience whistled and jeered; David Tudor prepared his own fixed realization of the solo, and every ensemble since has had to make its own decisions, as the Getty Research Institute's Scores Project documents with Tudor's worksheets.

#### Cent mille milliards de poèmes — Raymond Queneau (1961)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Chance & randomness, Co-authorship & derivatives
- Idea: Write the constraint so tightly that every reader's combination is a valid poem.
- What it is: A book of ten sonnets whose pages are cut into fourteen strips, one per line, so that readers can combine lines from different sonnets into 10^14 possible poems.
- Protocol: All ten sonnets share the same rhyme sounds and syntax, so any line can follow any line from another sonnet. The reader turns strips independently; each choice of fourteen strips is a new poem that Queneau never read.
- What the collective did: Readers assemble the poems; with 10^14 combinations, almost every reading produces a poem no one has read before. The book, designed by Robert Massin and published by Gallimard, led to the founding of Oulipo with François Le Lionnais, and has been translated and turned into online generators.

#### In C — Terry Riley (1964)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: Fixed order plus free timing turns an ensemble into a slowly shifting cloud of patterns.
- What it is: A one-page score of 53 short melodic patterns that any number of musicians play in order, each deciding when to move on, over a steady pulse on the note C.
- Protocol: "Any number of any kind of instruments can play." Each player repeats each pattern as many times as they like before moving to the next, stays within a few patterns of the others, and the piece ends when everyone has reached the last pattern.
- What the collective did: Premiered at the San Francisco Tape Music Center in November 1964; since then it has been played by rock bands, orchestras, school groups, the Malian and European musicians of Africa Express and ensembles such as BL!NDMAN and Third Coast Percussion, and the length and texture change with every group.

#### Musicircus — John Cage (1967)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Open participation, Chance & randomness, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Write only the conditions for a gathering and let simultaneity compose the piece.
- What it is: An event in which many invited musicians and performers play at the same time in one large space while the audience walks freely among them.
- Protocol: Cage's rule is an invitation: many performers agree to perform whatever they choose, simultaneously and in the same space, without coordination; the audience moves through the space and makes its own mix.

#### The Great Learning — Cornelius Cardew (1968)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Rules for listening to your neighbours can make a crowd of amateurs converge on a shared sound.
- What it is: A seven-part work (1968–71) setting Ezra Pound's translation of a Confucian text, written largely for untrained performers who sing, speak, drum and whistle by verbal and graphic rules.
- Protocol: In Paragraph 7, each singer chooses a starting note and sings a line of text on it a set number of times, then for each new line takes a note they can hear another singer holding; the choir drifts from a cluster toward a few shared pitches.

#### Les Moutons de Panurge — Frederic Rzewski (1969)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Open participation, Chance & randomness
- Idea: A rule that punishes mistakes by keeping them turns an ensemble's errors into the form of the piece.
- What it is: A piece for any number of musicians playing a 65-note melody additively (1, 1–2, 1–2–3 …), with optional non-musicians making noise alongside.
- Protocol: All players read the same melody, adding one note on each pass and then subtracting; the score instructs: "Stay together as long as you can, but if you get lost, stay lost."
- What the collective did: Performed by conservatories, bands and ensembles such as A Far Cry and eighth blackbird, sometimes with the audience as the non-musician section; the moment players drift apart and the resulting counterpoint differ in each performance.

#### Oblique Strategies — Brian Eno, Peter Schmidt (1975)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness, Rules & constraints
- Idea: A deck of rules turns chance into a working method that anyone can pick up.
- What it is: A boxed deck of cards, each printed with a short prompt, to be drawn at random when a creative process stalls; first published in 1975 and reissued in several editions.
- Protocol: When stuck, draw a card and follow it, however obscure, for example "Use an old idea" or "Consider different fading systems". The deck is sold to anyone; Eno and Schmidt do not see how it is used.
- What the collective did: Musicians, writers and designers have used the deck in their own work; Eno used it in studio sessions, including with David Bowie, and the publisher names Coldplay and Damon Albarn among users. Each draw produces a different decision in a different project.

#### Le Jeu de la Guerre (The Game of War / Kriegspiel) — Guy Debord (1977)
- Type: Game & experiment · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Instruction & score
- Idea: A rule set about supply lines: units that lose contact with their network cannot act.
- What it is: A two-player strategy game on a board of 25 by 20 squares, modelled on Clausewitzian warfare, in which armies must stay connected to their arsenals through lines of communication.
- Protocol: Each side has arsenals, forts, infantry, cavalry, artillery and relay units; lines of communication radiate from arsenals and relays; a unit cut off from them is paralysed; each turn allows up to five moves and one attack.
- What the collective did: Debord conceived the game in the 1950s and patented it in 1965; in 1977 he and Gérard Lebovici published the rules in French and English and had a few copies of the board made; in 1987 he and Alice Becker-Ho published a record of a complete game, and in 2008 the Radical Software Group released a networked version played online.

## Social Protocols & Economies

Rules for groups, exchanges and contracts in physical space: social sculpture, performance protocols, mail-art networks and artist-made economies.

### Social Sculpture & Collective Action

Works made by many people acting together under one rule: planting, walking, moving, gathering.

#### Théorie de la dérive (Theory of the Dérive) — Guy Debord (1956)
- Type: Score & instruction set · Substrate: Bodies & space · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Chance & randomness, Rules & constraints
- Idea: A walking protocol for groups that turns a city into a field of experiment.
- What it is: Debord's text sets out the dérive: small groups drifting rapidly through a city, letting its atmospheres, rather than errands, decide their path, and comparing notes afterwards.
- Protocol: Drift in several small groups of two or three people who share the same awareness; groups change composition between dérives; beyond about ten people the dérive splits; the average duration is one day, between two periods of sleep; impressions are cross-checked between groups.

#### Self Checkout — Jack Butcher (2025)

## Smart-Contract Art

Artworks whose rules run in a smart contract: supply, burning, merging, transfer, taxes and co-authorship enforced on-chain, and shaped by what collectors do.

### Supply, Burn & Merge

Open editions, burning, merging and composites: the final form is set by how many people buy, burn or combine.

#### Merge — Pak (2021)
- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Ethereum & L2s · Mechanisms: Merge & composite, Supply & scarcity, Circulation & passing on
- Idea: A game of extinction: a contract where every transfer can consume a token, so the market itself shrinks the work toward one survivor.
- What it is: An open sale of 'mass' on Nifty Gateway (2–4 December 2021). Each buyer's units became one dynamic token, and any two tokens that meet in the same wallet fuse, so the number of tokens only goes down.
- Protocol: During the 48-hour sale anyone could buy any number of mass units; at the end each collector's units were merged into one token. The contract lets an ordinary wallet hold only one token: when a second arrives, the larger mass absorbs the smaller and the smaller is burned (whitelisted addresses such as the Nifty Gateway omnibus are exempt). Each token has one of four classes, drawn as a white, yellow, blue-ringed or red-ringed sphere; the single largest mass is the 'alpha', drawn black with a white ring, and every sphere is sized relative to it.
- What the collective did: 28,983 collectors spent $91,806,519 on 312,686 units of mass, which became one token each (Nifty Gateway / Gemini). Secondary trading and deliberate merging then consumed tokens: by September 2026 the contract's totalSupply was 18,961, held by about 9,635 addresses (Blockscout).

#### Checks — Jack Butcher (2023)
- Type: Series & collection · Substrate: Ethereum & L2s · Mechanisms: Burn & destruction, Merge & composite, Supply & scarcity

- What it is: An $8 open edition of 80 checkmarks (January 2023), after Twitter began selling verification. From February 2023 collectors could destroy an edition to get an on-chain Original, then burn Originals into composites with fewer and fewer checks.

#### Async Art (programmable art) — Async Art (2020)
- Type: Platform & protocol · Substrate: Ethereum & L2s · Mechanisms: Co-authorship & derivatives, Contract & certificate, Market & exchange
- Idea: Split a picture into parts, sell the parts to different people, and let the picture be whatever their combined choices make it.
- What it is: An Ethereum platform where an artwork is issued as one Master token plus many Layer tokens. Whoever owns a Layer can switch it between states the artist defined, and the Master image is redrawn from all current choices.
- Protocol: The artist uploads a Master and its Layers and, for each Layer, fixes the allowed abilities (state change, move, rotate, colour, scale, reflect, visibility, opacity) and their ranges. - What the collective did: Collectors bought individual layers and changed them, so one Master could look different from week to week depending on many owners. Within its first year Async reports being featured at Christie's and selling single works in the six-figure range; in 2021 it extended the model to music stems.

- Type: Artwork · Substrate: Ethereum & L2s · Mechanisms: Burn & destruction, Contract & certificate, Supply & scarcity
- Idea: Put an artist's sale ritual in a contract: buyers must deal with the contract directly, and 'owning the immaterial' means burning the receipt.
- What it is: An ERC-20 token work deployed on 29 August 2017 and unveiled at InterAccess, Toronto. It restages Yves Klein's Zones of Immaterial Pictorial Sensibility, including a ritual any holder can perform to destroy their token.
- Protocol: IKB is sold only by calling the contract's buy() function (no front end, on purpose). ## Theory & Texts

Books, essays and research programmes that define protocols, systems and participation in art.

### Systems & Protocol Theory

Texts on systems aesthetics, protocols and control in networks.

#### Paragraphs on Conceptual Art — Sol LeWitt (1967)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Write the plan first; once it exists, the idea becomes a machine that makes the art.
- What it is: Essay published in the Summer 1967 issue of Artforum in which LeWitt defines Conceptual art: the idea is the most important part of the work, and its execution follows a plan fixed in advance.
- Protocol: All planning and decisions are made beforehand and 'the execution is a perfunctory affair'; the artist fixes a premise and a system, and anyone following it produces the work without new aesthetic choices.
- What the collective did: The text became the working manifesto for instruction-based practice: LeWitt's own wall drawings are still executed by drafters and museum teams from his written instructions, and later instruction, score and code-based artists cite it as a starting point.
- Text: https://www.artforum.com/features/paragraphs-on-conceptual-art-211354/ (Artforum 5, no. 10 (Summer 1967))
- Images: https://www.artforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/article00_large-51.jpg
- Project page: https://www.artforum.com/features/paragraphs-on-conceptual-art-211354/

#### Systems Esthetics — Jack Burnham (1968)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Open participation
- Idea: The artwork is not the object but the system of relations it sets in motion.
- What it is: Essay in Artforum (September 1968) arguing that art was shifting from making objects to organising systems: relations between people, materials, information and institutions over time.
- Protocol: Burnham borrows from systems theory and cybernetics: a work is a set of components and the rules of their interaction, and the artist designs those relations instead of a finished form.
- What the collective did: The essay gave critics and artists (Hans Haacke among them) a language for process, ecological and information-based work, and is now a standard reference for software, network and protocol art.
- Text: https://www.artforum.com/features/systems-esthetics-201372/ (Artforum 7, no. 1 (September 1968), pp. 30–35)
- Images: https://www.artforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/article00_large-23.jpg
- Project page: https://www.artforum.com/features/systems-esthetics-201372/

#### Sentences on Conceptual Art — Sol LeWitt (1969)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: Once the process is set, let it run: follow the rules even when the result surprises you.
- What it is: Thirty-five numbered statements first printed in the magazine 0 to 9 (no. 5, January 1969) and then in the first issue of Art-Language (May 1969), extending LeWitt's argument that ideas alone can be works of art.
- Protocol: The sentences state that the artist should not change the idea mid-course, that the process is mechanical once begun, and that 'the idea becomes a machine that makes the art'; perception of the work is left to each viewer.
- What the collective did: Reprinted in most Conceptual art anthologies, the sentences are read as a rulebook by artists who write systems for others to run, from wall-drawing assistants to generative and smart-contract artists.
- Text: https://monoskop.org/images/3/3d/LeWitt_Sol_1969_1999_Sentences_on_Conceptual_Art.pdf (0 to 9, no. 5 (January 1969); Art-Language 1, no. 1 (May 1969))

#### Software: Information Technology: Its New Meaning for Art — Jack Burnham (1970)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Open participation
- Idea: An exhibition can be a program: instructions that visitors and machines execute together.
- What it is: Exhibition and catalogue curated by Burnham at the Jewish Museum, New York (16 September – 8 November 1970), that showed conceptual artists and computer systems side by side, treating 'software' as instructions carried out by machines or by visitors.
- Protocol: Works were framed as 'software': sets of instructions written by the artist and run by a computer or by the public, such as Hans Haacke's Visitor's Profile, which tabulated visitors' answers to a questionnaire, and Ted Nelson's hypertext catalogue Labyrinth.
- What the collective did: Visitors' answers and actions supplied the content of several works; the show, which also travelled to the Smithsonian, is remembered as the first major US museum exhibition to bring the computer into the gallery.

#### Conceptual Art 1962–1969: From the Aesthetic of Administration to the Critique of Institutions — Benjamin H. D. Buchloh (1990)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Contract & certificate, Instruction & score
- Idea: Paperwork is a medium: certificates and contracts can carry the whole work.
- What it is: Essay in October (Winter 1990) that reads Conceptual art through its use of administrative forms: certificates, statements, lists, legal contracts and instructions.
- Protocol: Buchloh argues that artists such as LeWitt, Kosuth, Weiner and Haacke replaced craft with the procedures of bureaucracy, so that the work consists of rules, documents and institutional relations.
- What the collective did: The essay framed later readings of certificates, receipts and artist contracts as art, the lineage that curators and writers now connect to smart-contract art.

#### Is There Love in the Telematic Embrace? — Roy Ascott (1990)

- Idea: On a network, authorship is distributed: the artist sets up the link, the participants make the work.
- What it is: Essay in Art Journal (Autumn 1990) in which Ascott describes telematic art: works made by many participants linked through computer networks, with meaning produced in their exchanges.
- Protocol: The artist provides a networked context and a few rules for contribution; participants at distant nodes add, transform and pass on material, and no single author controls the result.
- What the collective did: The essay draws on Ascott's own networked projects, such as La Plissure du Texte (1983), in which participants in several cities wrote a fairy tale together; it became a founding text for telematic and net art and titles his collected writings, Telematic Embrace (2003).

#### Protocol: How Control Exists after Decentralization — Alexander R. Galloway (2004)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Voting & governance
- Idea: Protocol is how control works when there is no centre: design the protocol and you design the behaviour.
- What it is: Book (MIT Press, 2004) arguing that the internet is organised not by freedom but by protocols such as TCP/IP and DNS, which distribute control into technical standards; it also discusses net art and hacking as practices that work inside protocol.
- Protocol: Galloway defines protocol as a set of voluntary technical rules that every node must follow to connect; its power lies in making behaviour standard while leaving each node apparently free.
- What the collective did: The book became the main theory text for net art, tactical media and later crypto and protocol art, and is cited by artists who treat standards and smart contracts as their medium.

#### Words Made Flesh: Code, Culture, Imagination — Florian Cramer (2005)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Instruction & score, Rules & constraints
- Idea: A score, a spell and a program share one property: words that make things happen when executed.
- What it is: Book-length essay written at the Piet Zwart Institute in Rotterdam and published online in 2005, tracing the idea of executable language from magic and combinatory poetry to Fluxus scores and computer code.
- Protocol: Cramer shows that instructions have a long cultural history outside computing, and that the same text can be read as description or run as action, by people or by machines.
- What the collective did: Distributed free online and through the Internet Archive, the text became a common reference in software-art and code-poetry teaching.

- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Voting & governance
- Idea: To change a network, use its own rules against it.
- What it is: Book (University of Minnesota Press, 2007) arguing that networks create their own forms of control, both centralised and dispersed, and that resistance must work through 'exploits': holes found inside the protocol itself.
- Protocol: The authors describe networks as topologies governed by protocol, and the exploit as a practice that follows the rules exactly while turning them to unintended ends.

### Participation & Relational Art

#### Relational Aesthetics — Nicolas Bourriaud (1998)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Open participation, Market & exchange
- Idea: The artwork can be a situation: a set of conditions under which people meet and interact.
- What it is: Book first published in French as Esthétique relationnelle (Les presses du réel, 1998; English 2002) that describes 1990s art whose material is human relations: shared meals, meetings, services and temporary social spaces.

- What the collective did: The book named a generation (Rirkrit Tiravanija, Liam Gillick, Philippe Parreno and others) and became one of the most cited and most debated texts on participatory art.

- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Voting & governance

#### Protocol Futuring: Speculating Second-Order Dynamics of Protocols in Sociotechnical Infrastructural Futures — Botao Amber Hu (2025)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Rules & constraints, Time, decay & death, Co-authorship & derivatives
- Idea: Speculate on protocols, not products: watch how rules drift when they pass between groups and generations.
- What it is: CHI 2026 paper by Botao Amber Hu, Samuel Chua and Helena Rong that proposes 'protocol futuring', a design method that uses protocols as the material of speculative design, tested in a relay workshop on millennial-scale knowledge preservation.
- Protocol: In the Knowledge Futurama workshop, teams inherited partially formed protocol designs from earlier teams and had to reinterpret and extend them, a relay rule that made drift, jamming and adversarial readings visible.
- What the collective did: The multi-team relay produced protocols that changed through ambiguous handovers, shifting norms and simulated crises; the paper offers the method to HCI and design researchers studying long-lived infrastructures.

#### Protocol as Poetry: A Case Study of Pak's Smart Contract-Based Protocol Art — Botao Amber Hu (2025)
- Type: Book & essay · Substrate: Text & paper · Mechanisms: Contract & certificate, Open participation, Co-authorship & derivatives
- Idea: In protocol art the smart contract is the medium and collective participation is the message.
- What it is: Peer-reviewed paper (ARTECH 2025) that analyses Pak's smart-contract works, from The Fungible and Merge (2021) to Censored (2022) and Not Found (2023), and proposes seven characteristics of protocol art.
- Protocol: The paper names seven traits: system-centric composition, autonomous governance, distributed agency and communal authorship, temporal dynamism, economy-driven engagement, poetic messages embedded in interaction rituals, and composability.
- What the collective did: It is one of the first academic studies to use the term 'protocol art' (or 'protocolism') for smart-contract works and distinguishes it from conceptual, generative, participatory and performance art.
- Text: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12393 (ARTECH 2025, 12th International Conference on Digital and Interactive Arts)
